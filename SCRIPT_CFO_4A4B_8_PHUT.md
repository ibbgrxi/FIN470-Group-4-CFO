## GROUP 4A — MAIN PRESENTATION


### Slide 1: Introduction (0:00–0:12)

**Opening**

Good morning, everyone. I’m [Presenter Name], representing Group 4A and 4B in the role of VDG’s Chief Financial Officer. Today, I’ll explain our financial diagnosis and recommended actions for 2027.


### Slide 2: Strong Earnings, Weak Cash Conversion (0:12–1:00)

**Opening**

At first glance, VDG’s financial performance in 2026 looks very positive. Revenue increased by twenty percent, and net income grew by fifteen percent.

**Evidence**

However, this chart tells a different story. Operating cash flow fell from six hundred and twenty billion dong in 2025 to only two hundred and fifty billion in 2026 — a decline of almost sixty percent.

**Interpretation**

The cash conversion ratio also fell from over one hundred percent in 2024 to just twenty-one point seven percent in 2026. In other words, every one hundred dong of reported profit produced only about twenty-two dong of operating cash flow.

**Conclusion**

That is why we rate VDG’s earnings quality LOW for forecasting purposes. Now, let’s examine what happened to working capital.


### Slide 3: Working Capital Is Absorbing Cash (1:00–2:15)

**Opening**

The main problem is that growth is absorbing more working capital. Under accrual accounting, VDG can record a sale before it receives cash. It also needs to pay for inventory before that inventory is sold.

**Evidence**

In 2026, receivables increased by seventy-two point two percent, and inventory increased by forty-seven point eight percent, while revenue grew by only twenty percent.

**Chart**

As the chart shows, DSO rose from thirty-six point five days in 2024 to seventy-five point four days in 2026. DOH increased from seventy-eight point two to one hundred and eighteen point two days. Both trends mean more cash is tied up for longer.

**Limitation**

The full cash conversion cycle equals DSO plus DOH minus DPO. But DPO is not provided in the case, so we cannot calculate the actual CCC. Importantly, VDG’s 2026 operating cash flow is still positive — it is simply much weaker.

**Transition**

To address these pressures without damaging growth, we recommend three actions.


### Slide 4: Three Actions to Restore Liquidity (2:15–3:42)

**Goal**

As CFO, our goal is not to stop growth. It is to recover cash and finance expansion more responsibly.

**Action 1**

First, improve receivables collection through risk-based credit limits, weekly reviews of overdue accounts, and selective early-payment incentives. We propose reducing DSO to sixty-five days or less, without cutting credit to every distributor.

**Action 2**

Second, optimize inventory using SKU aging analysis, better demand forecasts, and focused action on slow-moving products. We propose reducing DOH to one hundred and five days or less, while protecting the supply of fast-selling items.

**Action 3**

Third, use a rolling thirteen-week cash-flow forecast, test both base and downside scenarios, and release capital expenditure in stages.

**Financial impact**

Under an illustrative scenario holding 2026 sales and cost of goods sold constant, the first two actions could release about four hundred and twenty-nine billion dong from receivables and three hundred and seventy-nine billion from inventory — around eight hundred and eight billion in total.

**Important note**

That is potential working-capital release, not guaranteed cash or new profit. We also need measurable targets to track implementation.


### Slide 5: 2027 CFO Dashboard & Decision (3:42–5:00)

**Opening**

For 2027, I recommend that the Board and investors monitor five financial indicators every quarter.

**KPI 1**

First, increase operating cash flow divided by net income from twenty-one point seven percent to at least sixty percent. This tests whether reported profit is turning into real cash.

**KPI 2 & 3**

Second, reduce DSO to sixty-five days or less. Third, reduce DOH to one hundred and five days or less. These indicators track collection and inventory efficiency.

**KPI 4 & 5**

Fourth, once DPO is verified, target at least a twenty-day reduction in the cash conversion cycle. Fifth, maintain a liquidity buffer above a Board-approved minimum, based on the thirteen-week cash forecast.

**Decision**

These are our proposed targets, not official VDG commitments. Our CFO recommendation is to APPROVE WITH CONDITIONS: verify cash collections, confirm inventory quality, and ensure adequate forecast liquidity before releasing each major investment stage.

**Transition**

Next, I’ll explain why reported earnings still need independent verification and how we should fund the expansion.


## GROUP 4B — SUPPLEMENT & CRITICAL ANALYSIS


### Slide 6: Are 2026 Earnings Really Sustainable? (5:00–6:25)

**Opening**

Before using 2026 profit as the foundation for expansion, I want to highlight three earnings-quality red flags.

**Red flag 1**

First, VDG recorded a three-hundred-and-twenty-billion-dong gain from selling an old warehouse. This gain is non-recurring, so we should not assume it will support next year’s earnings.

**Red flag 2**

Second, warranty expense fell from one point six percent of revenue to zero point nine percent, even though warranty claims did not decline significantly. Using the earlier expense rate would imply about one hundred and five billion dong of additional cost before tax. This is a sensitivity, not proven underprovisioning.

**Red flag 3**

Third, payment terms for selected distributors were extended to ninety to one hundred and twenty days, and some distributors can exchange slow-moving items. That raises questions about collections and sell-through.

**Balanced view**

Management may reasonably say longer credit supports market expansion and higher inventory prepares for future sales. However, receivables were already growing much faster than revenue in 2025, before the late-2026 credit extension.

**Evidence required**

We therefore request three evidence packages: receivables aging and subsequent collections; inventory aging, sell-through and exchange rights; and cash-flow and earnings reconciliations, including warranty assumptions and DPO. These concerns require verification; they do not prove fraud.


### Slide 7: Funding Growth with Financial Discipline (6:25–8:00)

**Opening**

The final question is how VDG should finance its 2027 expansion without putting liquidity under further pressure.

**Internal cash**

First, prioritize real internal cash generation through better collections and inventory management. Retained accounting profits do not automatically mean cash is available.

**Appropriate debt**

Second, consider debt only when the funding need and repayment capacity are supported by realistic cash forecasts. Short-term credit should cover temporary working-capital gaps; long-term investments need appropriately matched financing.

**Conditional equity**

Third, consider new equity if a justified long-term funding gap remains. Management must disclose the use of proceeds and potential dilution. The case does not provide enough data to recommend a specific debt-to-equity mix.

**Safeguards**

Before releasing major funds, we should verify customer collections, inventory quality and warranty estimates, and test the thirteen-week cash forecast under downside assumptions. We also recommend independent Audit and Risk review and quarterly disclosures of working-capital KPIs and recurring earnings.

**Closing**

To conclude, I support a phased 2027 expansion — but only with verified cash flows, suitable financing, and transparent governance. The final approval belongs to the Board, not the CFO. Our goal is sustainable growth supported by real cash generation. Thank you.
